\begin{abstract}
A multi-tier API gateway must decide, under bursty load and limited worker capacity, which request to serve next so that tiered service-level agreements (SLAs) are met without starving lower tiers. We compare seven online policies on identical workloads: FIFO, Earliest Deadline First (EDF), Deficit Round Robin (DRR), static EDF+DRR, the Apparent Tardiness Cost rule (ATC), a forecast-driven \emph{Predictive} EDF+DRR, and a new closed-loop \emph{Dynamic} EDF+DRR that (i) rescales DRR quanta by the criticality of pending requests and (ii) defers requests that can no longer meet their deadline. An exact mixed-integer linear program (MILP, single and multiple machines, warm-started, solved with HiGHS) provides optimality references on small batches. Evaluation covers eight synthetic scenarios (30 paired seeds each), the real Alibaba GenTD26 request trace across 1--32 workers, a chronological forecasting study, and ablations. Dynamic EDF+DRR cuts the SLA-violation rate versus static EDF+DRR in all eight scenarios (e.g.\ 53.3\%$\to$15.2\% under sustained overload, and 75.7\%$\to$42.4\% on GenTD26 with one worker), but it raises penalty-weighted tardiness in most non-overload scenarios; ATC attains the lowest weighted tardiness everywhere and is within a few percent of the MILP incumbent on small batches. The forecast-driven variant yields no statistically significant benefit over static EDF+DRR in any scenario, and produces schedules identical to it on GenTD26. We report these negative results alongside the positive ones and release all code and artifacts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multi-tier API gateways serve requests whose deadlines and penalties depend on the caller's service tier. During bursts, capacity forces scheduling decisions that determine which requests meet their deadlines and which tiers are served at all. Deadline-first policies protect urgent traffic but can starve other tiers and collapse under overload; fairness-first policies preserve service shares but ignore deadlines. Hybrids such as EDF within DRR tiers are natural, but it is unclear how much \emph{adaptation} of such a hybrid---by forecasting traffic, or by reacting to deadline pressure---is worth, and how far any online policy is from the offline optimum.

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Dynamic EDF+DRR}, a closed-loop hybrid with bounded urgency-driven quantum adaptation and late-work deferral, implemented with amortized $O(\log n)$ queue operations (Section~\ref{sec:dynamic}).
\item A \textbf{strong MILP benchmark}: a disjunctive formulation for $Rm\,|\,r_j|\sum w_jT_j$ on identical parallel machines, warm-started from the best heuristic, solved with HiGHS under a time limit, reporting the proven dual bound and whether optimality was proven (Section~\ref{sec:milp}).
\item A \textbf{controlled, paired comparison} of seven policies (plus ablations) on eight synthetic scenarios, the GenTD26 production trace, and MILP batches, with Student-$t$ confidence intervals and win/loss counts.
\item An honest account of \textbf{negative results}: forecast-driven quantum adaptation did not help, urgency-driven quanta alone hurt, and the benefit of the dynamic policy is an SLA-rate/weighted-tardiness trade-off rather than a uniform improvement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

EDF is optimal for preemptive uniprocessor deadline scheduling \cite{liu1973,dertouzos1974} but degrades sharply under overload, because it serves already-late requests first. Non-preemptive scheduling with release dates is NP-hard even for maximum lateness \cite{lenstra1977}, and total tardiness is NP-hard on one machine \cite{du1990}. Generalized processor sharing \cite{parekh1993} and Deficit Round Robin \cite{shreedhar1996} provide weighted fair service across flows with $O(1)$ work per decision; Jain's index \cite{jain1984} summarizes allocation equality. For weighted tardiness, the Apparent Tardiness Cost rule \cite{vepsalainen1987} is a standard dispatching heuristic, which we include as a strong non-hybrid baseline. Gradient-boosted trees \cite{chen2016} are a common forecasting baseline. We use the open-source HiGHS solver \cite{huangfu2018}. The GenTD26 trace comes from Alibaba's public generative-AI cluster-trace release \cite{gentd26}.

\section{System Model}

\subsection{Requests, tiers, and metrics}
A request $r$ has arrival time $a_r$, service time $s_r$, deadline $d_r$, SLA penalty $w_r$ (10/4/1 for VIP/Premium/Free), and a tier. Scheduling is non-preemptive on $W$ identical workers; service times are known at dispatch. A request completing at $C_r$ is \emph{late} if $C_r>d_r$, with tardiness $T_r=\max(0,C_r-d_r)$. We report:
\begin{itemize}
\item SLA violation rate (share of late requests);
\item penalty-weighted tardiness $\sum_r w_rT_r$ (the MILP objective);
\item mean/P95 waiting time and per-tier waiting and violation rates;
\item Jain's index \cite{jain1984} over the per-tier on-time rates, $J=(\sum_ix_i)^2/(n\sum_ix_i^2)$;
\item backlog at the horizon and scheduler runtime.
\end{itemize}
Because a policy that fails every tier equally has $J\approx1$, fairness must be read together with per-tier violation rates.

\subsection{Policies}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{FIFO}, \textbf{EDF} (global earliest deadline).
\item \textbf{DRR}: one FIFO queue per tier; quanta 0.60/0.30/0.15 (VIP/Premium/Free) in service-time units; a visited tier receives its quantum once and serves while its head request fits in the deficit counter.
\item \textbf{EDF+DRR}: DRR chooses the tier, EDF the request within the tier.
\item \textbf{ATC} \cite{vepsalainen1987}: serve the request maximizing $\frac{w_r}{s_r}\exp\!\big(-\max(d_r-s_r-t,0)/(k\bar s)\big)$, $k=2$, $\bar s$ the running mean service time.
\item \textbf{Predictive EDF+DRR}: quanta are scaled by a per-tier next-window demand forecast (Section~\ref{sec:forecast}) when the forecast exceeds 1.5$\times$ the recent mean, bounded by $1.2\times$. A backlog-proportional variant that needs no model is reported as ``proxy''.
\item \textbf{Dynamic EDF+DRR} (below).
\end{itemize}
All policies run in one discrete-event engine for any $W$; with $W=1$ it reproduces the dedicated single-server implementations schedule-for-schedule (enforced by unit tests). A previous multi-worker implementation we replaced never decremented the deficit counters, so DRR-family policies degenerated to plain round robin and the forecast-driven policy was indistinguishable from static EDF+DRR; this is fixed here.

\section{Dynamic EDF+DRR}
\label{sec:dynamic}

\textbf{Criticality.} For a pending request at time $t$ let $\sigma_r=d_r-t-s_r$ be its slack. Its criticality is $\kappa_r(t)=1/(1+\sigma_r/s_r)$ if $\sigma_r\ge0$ and $0$ otherwise: a request that cannot finish on time cannot be saved.

\textbf{Urgency-driven quanta.} At the start of each DRR visit, tier $i$ with $K$ tiers receives quantum
\begin{equation}
q_i' = q_i\Big(\varphi + K(1-\varphi)\frac{u_i}{\sum_ju_j}\Big),\qquad u_i=\sum_{r\in\text{tier }i}\kappa_r(t),
\end{equation}
with floor $\varphi=0.9$. Equal urgency reproduces $q_i$; no tier falls below $\varphi q_i$ (no starvation). $u_i$ is estimated from the first 64 heap entries of each tier. The SLA penalty is deliberately \emph{not} used as a weight ($w^0$): the base quanta already favor high tiers and a penalty weight double counts that priority.

\textbf{Late-work deferral.} Within a tier, EDF serves the earliest-deadline \emph{savable} request ($t+s_r\le d_r$); hopeless requests are served only when no savable request remains in the tier. This avoids the EDF overload ``domino effect'' at the cost of delaying hopeless requests. An optional aging limit serves a hopeless request after it is $L$ seconds late ($L=\infty$ by default).

\textbf{Complexity.} A request becomes hopeless exactly once as time advances, so three lazy-deletion heaps per tier (by deadline, by latest start, hopeless) give amortized $O(\log n)$ per operation. When service times far exceed the quanta, whole empty DRR cycles are skipped in closed form, which is exactly equivalent to iterating them.

\section{MILP Benchmark}
\label{sec:milp}

For a batch of $n$ requests on $m$ identical machines, let $S_j\ge a_j$ be start times, $T_j\ge S_j+s_j-d_j$, $T_j\ge 0$ tardiness, $b_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ for $i<j$ the ordering of a pair, and $x_{jk}\in\{0,1\}$ the machine assignment ($\sum_kx_{jk}=1$). Minimize $\sum_jw_jT_j$ subject to, for each $i<j$ and machine $k$,
\begin{align}
S_i+s_i &\le S_j + M(1-b_{ij}) + M(2-x_{ik}-x_{jk}),\\
S_j+s_j &\le S_i + Mb_{ij} + M(2-x_{ik}-x_{jk}),
\end{align}
with $M$ a horizon bound and request $j$ restricted to machines $0..j$ to break symmetry (for $m=1$ the assignment variables disappear). The model is solved by HiGHS, warm-started with the best heuristic schedule, under a time limit; we report the incumbent, the proven dual bound, and whether optimality was proven. The incumbent is never worse than the warm start, and idle time is removed by left-shifting. Instances (eight seeds of each of the \emph{overload}, \emph{tight\_deadlines}, \emph{vip\_spike}, and \emph{mixed\_burst} scenarios) are congested by compressing arrivals (factor 0.12) so that policies actually differ.

\section{Traffic Forecasting}
\label{sec:forecast}

A dependency-free per-tier ridge regressor predicts next-window request counts from the last three windows, their mean and trend, and cyclical time features, blended with a persistence forecast. We also train XGBoost \cite{chen2016} per tier. Models are trained on the first 60\% of a trace (chronologically), validated on the next 20\%, and tested on the last 20\%; scheduler experiments use a model trained on a held-out seed or on the first 40\% of GenTD26 and never on evaluation data. Spikes are defined from the six preceding windows ($>\mu+2\sigma$).

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Synthetic scenarios.} Eight scenarios, 1{,}000 requests each, one worker, 30 seeds, identical batches across policies: \emph{normal} ($\rho\approx0.7$), \emph{overload} ($\rho\approx1.15$), \emph{vip\_spike}, \emph{free\_spike}, \emph{mixed\_burst} (periodic 15 s bursts every 60 s), \emph{random\_burst} (unforecastable burst times), \emph{tight\_deadlines} and \emph{relaxed\_deadlines}.

\textbf{GenTD26.} The \texttt{lora\_request\_trace.csv} requests (26{,}392 usable rows) are mapped to the request model by documented rules: relative arrival time, execution time as service time, SHA-256 hashing of \texttt{groupId} to tiers (20/30/50\%), deadlines $2\times/4\times/8\times$ service time (0.25 s minimum slack), penalties 10/4/1. Tiers, deadlines and penalties are \emph{modeling assumptions}, not observed production facts. For the capacity sweep ($W\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$) the horizon is 1{,}750{,}000 s (24{,}522 offered requests). For paired statistics, the 60\% of the trace following the forecaster's training prefix is cut into six chronological segments.

\textbf{Statistics.} Differences are paired per seed/segment; we report the mean difference, a 95\% Student-$t$ interval, and win/loss/tie counts. ``Better''/``worse'' means the interval excludes zero; ``n.s.'' otherwise.

\subsection{Predictive (forecast-driven) EDF+DRR}

The forecast-driven policy is statistically indistinguishable from static EDF+DRR in 14 of 16 scenario/metric cells of Table~\ref{tab:syn_paired}; in the other two it is slightly \emph{worse} with a confidence interval excluding zero (SLA rate $+0.003$ under overload and $+0.002$ in \emph{vip\_spike}, both of negligible size). On GenTD26 its schedules are identical to static EDF+DRR in 6/6 segments (0 wins, 0 losses, 6 ties). In a diagnostic run on the first 5{,}000 test requests, none of 18{,}072 DRR visits changed any quantum: the forecast never exceeded the $1.5\times$ spike threshold that triggers adaptation. One configuration does favor the forecast: in an extreme-overload stress test (20 requests/s, $\rho\approx3.5$, 1{,}000 requests, 20 s horizon, 30 seeds, held-out training seed, 1 s windows) the forecast-driven policy shows a small paired reduction in mean wait ($-0.17$ s, 95\% CI $[-0.24,-0.10]$, 24/30 wins) and SLA rate ($-0.008$, CI $[-0.014,-0.003]$, 21/30 wins). That test counts only requests completed before the horizon, so it is subject to survivorship bias, and we have not established the mechanism; we therefore treat it as a hypothesis for follow-up rather than evidence that forecasting helps (\texttt{python -m src.evaluate\_research}). The model-free backlog-proportional proxy is significantly \emph{worse} than static EDF+DRR in seven of eight scenarios (e.g.\ 74.7\% vs.\ 53.3\% SLA violations under overload).

Table~\ref{tab:sens} separates the two mechanisms. The SLA-rate gain comes entirely from \emph{deferral}: deferral alone (no aging limit) lowers the violation rate by 0.178, while urgency-driven quanta with a low floor ($\varphi=0.25$) roughly triple weighted tardiness. Raising the floor to $\varphi=0.9$ makes quantum adaptation nearly neutral, so we keep it as a bounded safeguard rather than a source of improvement. The aging limit $L$ trades the two objectives: $L=0.25$ s yields a 7.6\% tardiness increase for a 0.9-point SLA gain, whereas $L=\infty$ gives the largest SLA gain (17.8 points) at 31\% higher tardiness (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). \subsection{Runtime}
On the full GenTD26 trace with one worker, scheduling takes 0.13 s (EDF+DRR), 0.52 s (deferral only), 1.3 s (ATC) and 5.7 s (Dynamic), against 13.7 s for the forecast-driven policy in our implementation, which recomputes features for every DRR visit. All are far below the trace duration.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{H1 (EDF beats FIFO under overload).} Partly. EDF has far lower weighted tardiness than FIFO in every non-trivial scenario, but under sustained overload its SLA-violation rate (92.3\%) is not lower than FIFO's (92.1\%).
\textbf{H2 (DRR gives more balanced tier completion than EDF).} Not supported with the default weighted quanta: DRR prioritizes by quantum weight and shifts backlog to Free. A fairness-oriented quantum profile exists in the code (0.20/0.40/0.60) but was not evaluated here.
\textbf{H3 (EDF+DRR better SLA/fairness trade-off than EDF or DRR alone).} Supported on SLA metrics under load (consistently better than both), not at light load, where EDF wins.
\textbf{H4 (capacity boundary).} Supported on GenTD26.
\textbf{H5 (forecast improves surge handling).} Not supported by the scenario study or GenTD26; only the extreme-overload stress test shows a small, unexplained effect. \textbf{H6 (no benefit when unforecastable)} is trivially consistent, but uninformative because there was no benefit to lose.
\textbf{New finding.} Closed-loop deadline awareness (Dynamic EDF+DRR) delivers the large, reproducible gains in SLA violation rate and mean wait that forecasting did not, but not in weighted tardiness, where ATC dominates.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} (i) Tier, deadline, and penalty assignments for GenTD26 are synthetic; the trace's real SLAs are unknown. (ii) Service times are assumed known at dispatch. (iii) Workers are identical and non-preemptive; GPU batching is not modeled. (iv) MILP results are limited to $n\le18$ and are not proven optimal beyond $n=14$ on one machine. (v) Dynamic EDF+DRR was tuned (floor, aging) on seeds disjoint from the evaluation seeds, but on the same synthetic family. (vi) Only one production trace is used.

\section{Conclusion}

Among online policies, deadline-aware deferral of hopeless work is the single most effective SLA-rate improvement we found, and it composes cleanly with DRR tier isolation; weighted-tardiness minimization is better served by ATC. Forecast-informed quantum adaptation, at least with the small models and demand signals studied here, did not improve on static EDF+DRR, and an exact MILP shows the remaining heuristic-to-optimal gap is small for ATC/EDF on small batches. Future work includes SLA-count MILP references, preemption and batching, fairness-quanta sweeps, and additional production traces.
